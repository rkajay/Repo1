# Porter's Five Forces — Reference

Used to assess the structural attractiveness of an *industry* (not a single company). Each force affects how much of the value created in the industry incumbents can capture as profit.

## The five forces

### 1. Threat of new entrants
Driven by barriers to entry: capital requirements, economies of scale, brand loyalty/switching costs, access to distribution, proprietary technology or IP, regulatory/licensing barriers, and expected retaliation from incumbents.
Research targets: recent new entrants and their funding/traction, capital intensity (e.g. typical startup cost), regulatory licensing requirements, patent landscape.
High threat → unfavorable for incumbent profitability. Low threat (high barriers) → favorable.

### 2. Bargaining power of suppliers
Driven by supplier concentration vs. buyer (industry) concentration, switching costs to change suppliers, availability of substitute inputs, supplier's ability to forward-integrate, and how critical/differentiated the input is.
Research targets: concentration of the supply base, any recent supplier price increases or consolidation, unionization (for labor as a supplier), single-source dependencies.
High supplier power → unfavorable for industry profitability.

### 3. Bargaining power of buyers
Driven by buyer concentration vs. seller concentration, price sensitivity, switching costs, buyer's ability to backward-integrate, and availability of full information/price transparency to buyers.
Research targets: customer concentration (is revenue concentrated in a few large buyers?), price transparency tools/marketplaces in the industry, evidence of buyer consolidation.
High buyer power → unfavorable for industry profitability.

### 4. Threat of substitute products or services
Not direct competitors, but different products satisfying the same underlying need. Driven by relative price-performance of substitutes, switching costs, and buyer propensity to substitute.
Research targets: adjacent categories gaining share at this industry's expense, technology shifts that could obsolete the offering, price trends of substitutes.
High substitute threat → unfavorable for industry profitability.

### 5. Rivalry among existing competitors
Driven by number and balance of competitors, industry growth rate (slow growth → fight for share), fixed/storage costs (pressure to fill capacity), product differentiation, exit barriers, and strategic stakes.
Research targets: market share distribution (is it fragmented or concentrated?), recent price wars or margin compression, M&A consolidation activity, capacity utilization trends.
High rivalry → unfavorable for industry profitability.

## Synthesis guidance
After rating all five, identify the **one or two dominant forces** — the ones actually constraining profitability in this industry today, not all five treated as equally important (rarely true in practice). Then state the strategic implication: e.g. "with low entry barriers and high buyer power, sustainable advantage will come from switching-cost-creating features or scale-driven cost leadership, not from initial product differentiation alone."

## Common pitfalls to avoid
- Don't analyze "competitors" only under rivalry — entrants and substitutes are also competitive threats, just structurally distinct ones.
- Don't treat this as a company analysis — it's an industry analysis. If the user wants company-specific strategic position, pair with SWOT.
- Avoid generic textbook claims ("there is always some rivalry") without industry-specific evidence.
